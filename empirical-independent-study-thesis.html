Research

From Awareness to Conversion: How Firm Characteristics Shape Advertising Budget Allocation Across the Marketing Funnel

At a glance

  • Focus Economic modeling and empirical analysis of advertising budget allocation across the marketing funnel
  • Advisor Dr. Colin Davison, Department of Economics
  • Method A hand-collected 18-firm panel, an imputed outcome measure, and panel regressions (pooled and firm fixed-effects)
  • Sample 18 large, publicly listed firms, 7 industries, 270 firm-year observations, 2010–2024
  • Headline Firm size and industry are the strongest, most consistent predictors of how much of a budget goes toward brand-building versus conversion. Profitability and cash on hand aren't.
Download full paper (PDF)

Advertising has always struck me as one of the more interesting corners of economics, it's where firm behavior, consumer psychology, and game theory collide, but it's also genuinely a design discipline, the details and aesthetics of a campaign matter as much as the math behind it. What pulled me toward this specific question was the marketing funnel itself. Once you stop treating advertising as one undifferentiated activity and see it as awareness, consideration, and conversion, each with its own logic, a much harder question opens up: how does a firm actually decide where in that funnel to spend, and why would that decision look different for a small retailer than for a large tech platform? That's the question this thesis tries to answer.

The question, in plain terms

Every company that advertises faces some version of the same tradeoff: how much of the budget goes toward building awareness (the kind of advertising that keeps a brand on people's minds) versus how much goes toward pushing people who are already interested to actually buy (the kind built for clicks and conversions). Both matter, but they trade off against each other, and the right balance almost certainly isn't the same for every company. This thesis asks a direct question: does that split depend on who the firm is, its size, its financial position, the industry it competes in, and if so, how?

How I actually built it

The hard part of this project wasn't the modeling, it was the data. No public database tracks how companies split their ad budgets between brand and performance marketing, so I built the dataset by hand: 15 years (2010–2024) of advertising spend, revenue, profit, assets, debt, and inventory data, pulled directly from the annual reports of 18 large, publicly listed companies across 7 industries, names like P&G, Unilever, Coca-Cola, Amazon, Ford, and Pfizer. That's 270 firm-year observations, each one extracted and standardized by hand rather than pulled from a commercial dataset.

Since no company reports its actual upper-funnel-versus-lower-funnel split, I couldn't just look this number up either, I had to construct it. I used the theoretical model itself to build a stand-in: for each firm-year, I took its advertising spend relative to its peers in the same year, then mapped that through a calibrated logistic curve to produce a plausible upper-funnel share. It's an imputed measure, not a directly reported one, and I'm upfront about that limitation below, but it's grounded in the same theory the rest of the thesis builds on, not an arbitrary proxy.

What the data showed

Running that panel through two models, one comparing firms and industries against each other, one tracking how the same firm's allocation shifts as it changes over time, a few patterns held up consistently.

Size matters more than anything else. Larger firms consistently devote a bigger share of their budget to the upper funnel, and this holds both when comparing different firms and when tracking the same firm as it grows. It's the single most stable relationship in the entire analysis.

Industry matters almost as much. FMCG and Tech firms allocate meaningfully more toward brand-building than the sample average, while Retail and Leisure firms lean the other way, toward conversion-focused spending, even after controlling for financial differences. That tracks with intuition: a consumer goods brand or a platform business lives and dies by staying top of mind, while a retailer is often trying to close a sale right now.

Leverage has a smaller, positive effect that shows up consistently but modestly across every version of the model.

Profitability, cash on hand, and inventory turnover showed no consistent relationship with funnel allocation at all. That was genuinely one of the more interesting findings, the theory would suggest more profitable, more liquid firms have more room to invest in long-horizon brand building, but in this sample, year-to-year swings in profitability and cash simply didn't move the needle. Whatever determines a firm's funnel strategy, it looks more like a stable, structural decision than something that gets adjusted based on how the last year went.

For the economically inclined

This section gets into the theoretical model behind the empirical work above, feel free to skip it if you're here for the findings.

The empirical results above are a reduced-form test of a more formal argument developed earlier in the thesis: a multi-stage extension of the classic Dorfman–Steiner advertising model, where each funnel stage (awareness, consideration, conversion) has its own logit response function, and the parameters of that function are shaped by the firm's own characteristics. The punchline of that model is this: optimal allocation equalizes marginal profit per dollar spent across every stage, given each firm's own effectiveness parameters, meaning a firm's optimal upper-funnel share isn't universal, it's a function of who the firm is. The regressions are, in effect, asking whether that theoretical prediction shows up in how real firms actually spend, and largely, in the dimensions of scale and industry, it does.

What it doesn't claim

A few limitations are worth stating plainly, partly because they were front of mind while writing this, and partly because I think knowing what a piece of research doesn't show is as important as what it does. The sample only covers large, publicly listed advertisers, so these patterns may not hold for smaller or privately held firms with very different constraints. The core outcome variable, upper-funnel share, is imputed from theory rather than directly observed, so the results speak to a theory-consistent proxy, not firms' actual internal budget splits. And the dataset, while real and hand-built, is still a relatively small, annual-frequency panel with a narrow set of covariates, it leaves out a lot of the day-to-day, channel-level detail that likely also shapes funnel strategy. I'd treat this as a solid first look at how observable firm traits line up with a theory-driven view of the funnel, not a final word on it.
