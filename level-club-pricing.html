Pricing Strategy: Moving a Casablanca Billiard Club to Hourly Pricing

At a glance

  • Business Level Club, a two-floor billiard club and café in Casablanca, one of the first in Morocco to offer American pool tables
  • Role Marketing & Growth Intern, Jun – Jul 2023
  • Change Replaced per-game pricing with the club's first hourly rate, launched with a “buy 2 hours, get the 3rd free” offer
  • Measurement Redemption-based tracking (customers showed the Instagram post or brought a flyer), compared against the club's usual occupancy and sales
  • Results Customer visits up about 50%; sales up about 30% during the promotion

The business

Level Club is a large billiard club on two floors, with snooker, English pool, and American pool tables, plus a café serving drinks, waffles, and crêpes. It was one of the first clubs in Morocco to offer American pool.

It was also built to be a different kind of club. The typical Moroccan pool hall is small, offers only English pool and snooker, and draws a mostly male crowd. Level Club set out to be a place where friends spend an evening around pool and where players come to practice, not just to play strangers: more upscale, and safer and more welcoming for women and groups. Prices were set slightly above nearby clubs to match that positioning.

Why hourly pricing

Pool halls in Morocco typically charge per game, and the loser pays. That model suits head-to-head play between strangers, not the experience Level Club was selling. An hourly rate charges for what the club actually offers: time at a table.

It fit the club in several ways. Groups that take their time pay for the time they use, and slow games no longer tie up tables without adding revenue. Practice becomes something the club can sell, since a player training alone has no game to pay for. Longer stays also support the café. Because the change fit the club's value proposition, it was an easy case to make to the owner.

Designing the launch offer

We launched the hourly rate with a promotion: buy 2 hours, get the 3rd free. Its main job was to bring people in to try the new pricing; a second goal was to fill tables on the days and hours that the owner's occupancy data showed were usually quiet.

I chose this over “buy 1 hour, get 1 free” on purpose, because people tend to overestimate how long they'll play when they start a session. The offer looked generous, but anyone taking it paid for two hours, and few used the full third. In pricing terms, it was a quantity discount: the free hour made the offer attractive, and the two paid hours set the minimum spend.

Promotion and measurement

The offer ran on Instagram and on flyers. To redeem it, customers had to show the Instagram post or bring a flyer, which tied each redemption to the channel that brought it in. To measure impact, I compared visits and sales against the owner's averages for table occupancy by day and season, along with what I saw on the floor during the internship.

Results

Customer visits up about 50% compared with the club's usual levels for the season.

Sales up about 30% while the promotion ran.

A longer run than planned. Designed as a short launch offer, the promotion ran for one to two months.

Repeat visits. Groups rarely stayed the full three hours, but they came back.

These are estimates from a two-month internship, compared against seasonal averages rather than a controlled test.

What I took from it

The structure of an offer can matter more than the size of the discount. Designing it around how customers plan their time, and building measurement into it from the start, is what made the change both effective and measurable.
