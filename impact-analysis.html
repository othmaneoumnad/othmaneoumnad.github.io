Research

Social Entrepreneurship Program: 20-Year Impact Analysis

At a glance

  • Focus A program evaluation assessing the long-term outcomes of two decades of nonprofit consulting work
  • Program Social Entrepreneurship (SE), College of Wooster's 20-year program pairing student teams with nonprofits to build real business and revenue strategies
  • Method Review of past business plans and reports, interviews with former client organizations, transcript coding, and financial filing (Form 990) analysis, conducted with a faculty-advised research team
  • Scope 40 client organizations since 2006, with an in-depth study across 6 organizations and 8 projects
  • Headline 77% of program-recommended projects were still implemented years later

The program

The Social Entrepreneurship program has spent two decades pairing Wooster student teams with nonprofits and community organizations across Northeast Ohio, delivering business plans, feasibility studies, and revenue strategies for organizations that could rarely afford that kind of research on their own. To mark the program's 20th year, my team and I were tasked with turning that same research discipline back on the program itself: had two decades of this work actually held up?

Our approach

We treated this the way any credible impact evaluation should be treated, as a question that required both a qualitative and a quantitative answer, neither sufficient on its own.

The starting point was the archive itself. Before a single interview was conducted, my team and I worked back through years of past deliverables, business plans, feasibility studies, final reports, to establish a baseline: what had actually been recommended to each organization, and what would count as evidence that it had taken hold.

From there, we reached out to leaders at a sample of past client organizations and conducted semi-structured interviews, giving each conversation enough structure to be comparable across organizations while leaving room for what we hadn't thought to ask. Every interview was recorded and transcribed in full.

The transcripts were then themed: read closely, coded against a shared framework, and organized into a structured spreadsheet alongside the supporting quotes behind each theme, so that every conclusion could be traced back to its source. In parallel, we pulled Form 990 filings for each organization, comparing financial performance before and after their SE engagement to see whether proposed revenue strategies had actually materialized in the numbers, not just in the plan.

What surfaced in the process

Patterns emerged quickly once the coding was underway, capacity constraints, leadership turnover, the gap between a good plan and the resources to execute it. But one line from an interview cut through more directly than the framework itself: "Nonprofits have no money." No elaboration necessary. It became something of a north star for interpreting everything else we found, a reminder that behind every implementation metric was an organization simply trying to do more with less.

The bigger picture

Taken together, the qualitative and financial analysis pointed to a program with real, durable impact, both in how often recommendations were put into practice and in the revenue they generated for the organizations that adopted them, results that, together with the rest of the team's findings, were substantial enough to be featured in the program's own account of its 20-year history.

Recognized by the program

This research was featured in the SE program's official write-up commemorating its 20th anniversary, published on the program's blog, which previewed these findings publicly and credited the student research team behind them: Assessing 20 Years of Impact
